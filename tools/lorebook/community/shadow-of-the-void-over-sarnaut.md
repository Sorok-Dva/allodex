## Part 1

### The Council of the Heads of the Orders

The air in the Council Hall was thick and heavy, as though it had been cast from lead. It did not move, did not stir in any draught — the tall arched windows had been sealed tight, so that no chance glance, no stray sound could disturb those present. The light that came from the cold spheres floating beneath the very dome was harsh and merciless. It picked details out of the half-dark: the worn velvet upholstery of the gigantic round table, the veins in the marble of the columns, the weary faces of those who sat there.

Here, in the very heart of the allod of Ney, the domain of the Historians, where the ley lines of the world converged, three had gathered. Three whose will decided the fate of the allods.

Zayan, head of the Order of Balance, sat motionless, his fingers laced together before him. His face, with its regular, severe features, showed neither anger nor irritation.

"Your reckless support of Nefer Ur's insane experiments," his voice was quiet, but every word fell on the table like a heavy stone, "has brought all of Sarnaut to the brink of destruction. You did not simply make a mistake. You violated the fundamental balance. And everyone will have to pay for it. From the lowliest day labourer in the harbour to us, sitting in this hall."

Catherina, whose night-black hair and young face made one forget her age — though its shadow could be glimpsed in the depths of her bottomless eyes — smirked. The light, almost weightless hand waved the elf's words away like a bothersome fly.

"Moving forward always carries a risk, Zayan," her voice sounded pragmatic and cold, like the ticking of machinery. "Clinging to an outdated, outworn order — that is what is truly criminal. We are building the future, not guarding a museum piece covered in the dust of superstition."

"Enough," Zayan's quiet voice cut the silence like a blade. "Your games have gone too far. Too far even for you, Catherina."

"'Games', Zayan? You were always conservative. You call the inevitable march of progress a game? A step of the future that can be stopped neither by your prohibitions nor by your fears."

"Progress?" Zayan took a step forward. His shadow lengthened and fell over Catherina. "You would call these insane experiments progress? You, whose Order of Progress took him under its wing, gave him resources, turned a blind eye to his antics? You saw his designs, Catherina! You knew what he was striving for!"

"He was striving for a breakthrough!" Catherina retorted, and the fire of fanatical stubbornness flared in her eyes. "For overcoming the barriers that people like you erect around knowledge! Yes, there was a risk. Risk is the price of evolution. The future is not built on caution, Zayan. It is bought with courage — sometimes desperate courage."

The third person in the hall raised her hand. Her movements were smooth and soothing, as though she were smoothing out the invisible fabric of the universe. Alice, also known as Klavdia Kalugina. Head of the Order of Creation. Her simple garments of unbleached linen seemed out of place in this seat of power, but her calm was unshakeable.

"An argument will not lead us to the truth," her voice was warm as a summer wind, yet a will of steel could be felt in it. "Nefer Ur acted within what was permitted. His intentions were… pure. He wished to create. To create something new."

"And what was he creating, Klavdia?" Zayan turned to her, and his cold anger found a new target. "What was he creating, I ask? Because the fruits of his 'creation' we are now witnessing all over the world! Anomalies of space. The chaotic growth of allods. Distortions and substitutions of reality everywhere!"

"There is no creation without mistakes," Klavdia would not give in, though her eyes grew dim. "It is a process. We learn. We err. We correct. My Order, among others, is correcting."

"There is nothing left to correct!" Zayan's voice rose a tone. "Because the consequences of his 'mistake' have materialised! You wanted something new? Here it is!"

With a sharp movement he unfolded a projection in the centre of the hall, showing the current map of the Astral. Chaos incarnate, a raging sea. And at the centre of the storm — an island. A phantom island, covered with distorted, broken vegetation and buildings. And on it — a castle. A citadel with towers twisted into unnatural spirals, with windows that held no light, only an all-consuming blackness.

"Quator. Schlegerlogt," Zayan hissed. "It exists. It is no phantom, no mirage. It is material."

Catherina wanted to object, but Zayan stopped her with a look.

"This is not simply a random fragment of the past cast up by the current of time. Its appearance is a symptom. The direct consequence of Horizon — which was meant to remain a prophetic nightmare, a tale to frighten children — taking shape and becoming part of our reality. Your 'progress', Catherina, your 'creation', Klavdia, have torn the veil."

He paused, letting the words reach their mark.

"There is a prophecy. A prophecy of the 'end of times'. It says that the world will be swallowed not by darkness, not by fire, not by ice. By the Void. Come from outside. Many confuse the Void with Darkness. Do not make that mistake. Darkness is part of the universe, as natural as Light. The Void is not a force. It is anti-being. The negation of all that exists. Not-life. Not-death. Nothing. And now, thanks to your actions, the prophecy of Nihaz, created to instil fear in us, has regained its power. Horizon has become reality, and the events the prophets foretold have become possible. Most likely, inevitable."

Silence hung over the hall. Even Catherina found nothing to say. Klavdia turned pale, her lips trembled.

And it was she, the head of the Order of Creation, who broke the silence, taking a step no one could have foreseen.

"If Darkness is part of being, as you claim, Zayan," her voice grew stronger, a steely resolve rang in it, "then its voice must be heard here and now. Otherwise our council is incomplete. Our decision is flawed. We condemn what we do not understand, we deny what we do not wish to speak with."

She looked around at her astonished companions.

"I have invited the one whose opinion we have ignored for centuries. The one whose knowledge may be the only thing able to shed light on this… Void."

The shadow by the far wall stirred, separated from the general gloom and stepped into the circle of light. The air in the hall instantly grew cold. Frost appeared on the stone flags of the floor.

A woman emerged from the shadows. Tall, imperious, clad in robes of the purest night velvet strewn with tiny silver stars, like a reflection of the night sky. Her face was beautiful and impassive, like a statue of black marble. And her eyes… her eyes were bottomless, and whole universes swirled in them — but dead universes, cold, deprived of the warmth of light.

"Sister?" Klavdia whispered, and pain, pride and fear sounded in her voice.

"I need no introduction," Hirkalla's voice was like the rustle of silk over a bare blade. It did not sound so much as envelop the mind. "You know me. You always have. You preferred to pretend that I did not exist. That we did not exist. That the power I represent must be banished, suppressed, forgotten. I am the head of what will now be called the Order of Mysteries. Though your name is but a pitiful attempt to label what is beyond your control."

She walked up to the table and soundlessly took the fourth seat.

"Darkness cannot have a voice in the fate of the world!" Zayan declared.

"It can," Hirkalla retorted. "Because without Darkness there is no Light. Without silence there is no word. Without rest there is no action. You, the keeper of Balance, should understand this better than anyone. You fight imbalance? Here it is — before you. An imbalance created by your fear. I do not offer destruction. I offer… restructuring. A new hierarchy. Recognition."

Catherina watched all this with suddenly awakened interest. Sparks of calculation lit up in her eyes. A new power. A new player. New opportunities for study, for progress.

"She is right, Zayan," Catherina said unexpectedly. "It is logical. If Darkness is part of the system, it must be integrated, not locked away in a dungeon. A locked-up force always finds its way out in an explosion."

The tension in the Council Hall reached its peak. Four leaders, four embodiments of irreconcilable philosophies, froze in silent confrontation. The cold from Hirkalla struggled with the warmth emanating from Klavdia; Zayan's merciless logic clashed with Catherina's icy pragmatism.

It was Zayan, the keeper of the most fragile of concepts, who broke the pause.

"A dispute by force is pointless. A dispute by words is useless," he said. His eyes were closed, as though he were listening to the unseen scales of fate. "But inaction is deadly. One thing remains. An ancient solution. Tested over the centuries, whenever the elders could not come to an agreement. A tournament."

"A tournament?" Catherina snorted sceptically. "Exhibition bouts between champions? That is primitive."

"No," Zayan shook his head. "Not primitive. It is archetypal. It is the foundation of the universe — the trial of strength, of will, of spirit."

Catherina raised an eyebrow. A smirk played on her lips.

"Duels?"

"Not duels," Zayan cut her off sharply. "A trial. The strength of ideas must be proven by those who carry them. Not by us, mired in dogma and ambition, but by those who are still pure of heart and strong of spirit. Those who believe in our principles not in word, but in deed. Let the will and wit of the champions determine whose path Sarnaut needs most now. Whose philosophy can stand against the approaching end. We shall put forward champions. Each Order — its best. They will meet on neutral ground. On my allod, Ney."

"Why not on Ammer?" Klavdia asked quickly.

"Ammer is no longer neutral," Zayan snapped. "Ney is my personal allod, a place of power of Balance. Its laws suppress any excessive manifestation of any one of the forces. That is the guarantee of fairness. Besides," he glanced again at the projection of Schlegerlogt, "Ammer may be… unsafe. Ney is protected by ancient barriers."

Hirkalla nodded silently. For her this was a chance. A chance to legitimise her Order, to show its strength, to draw to her side those disillusioned with the old order.

Catherina saw in it a priceless testing ground for her new developments and an opportunity to lure away talent.

Klavdia — an opportunity to atone for her guilt and prove that her path of Creation was right.

Zayan — the only way to resolve the crisis without an immediate apocalypse, and to test whose understanding of balance was true.

"I agree," Hirkalla was the first to break the silence.

"The Order of Progress accepts the challenge," Catherina replied almost at once.

Hirkalla seemed to be relishing the moment.

"At last," she rustled, "you propose something truly interesting. Not tedious debates, but a game with everything at stake. Yes. I agree."

All eyes turned to Klavdia. She closed her eyes for a moment, as though listening to something, and opened them.

"So be it. The Order of Creation takes part."

Zayan slowly exhaled.

"The champions must be assembled by the full moon. The rules will be announced on the spot."

He turned to the window, his back to them. The conversation was over.

Three figures silently left the hall. Catherina — with a firm, decisive step; Klavdia — thoughtful and sad; Hirkalla — dissolving into the shadows from which she had appeared.

Zayan remained alone in the huge, suddenly empty hall. He was looking at the raging image of the phantom island.

"The mechanism has been set in motion," he whispered into the silence. "And its consequences cannot be foretold by any prophecy."

## Part 2

The harbour of the allod of Ney had known quieter days. The air trembled with the low hum of flywheels turning somewhere in the depths of the structures, and it smelled of thunderstorms, oil and cold stone. One after another, ships moored at the piers.

Here, to this heart of Balance, came those who were destined to decide the outcome of the dispute of the Four.

The first to step off a massive floating barge was a silent orc in a hooded travelling cloak. He carried no visible weapon, but his gait, light and noiseless, betrayed an experienced warrior. His gaze, gliding over the pier from beneath the hood, was quick, all-encompassing and expressionless. He stepped aside, leaned against the wall and went still, merging with the shadow, simply watching. Skulnar the Fierce. The one who was not supposed to exist.

Another notable figure was coming down the opposite gangway. Priden Murk, the famous traveller and pathfinder. He trod the stone with innate caution, adjusting gloves of the softest leather. Seeing each other, the two shook hands — they had known each other for a long time.

But the guests kept arriving.

The silence was immediately broken by a noisy, bustling band that poured out onto the pier. They pointed at the flying cargo platforms, argued about how the steam golems worked, and nearly came to blows over a cogwheel found on the pavement. At their head walked a lass with clever, quick eyes and the confidence of a born leader. Gerda, and with her Trondur and Wickham, the Sharps.

Quietly, as though afraid of crushing someone, Waterlily stepped ashore. Her bare feet touched the stone softly, almost soundlessly. But wherever her heel touched the surface, a tiny, unknown flower sprouted for a moment, only to wither a second later and crumble to dust. She walked on, bearing with her a quiet sadness and the scent of distant meadows.

Eurial came down the gangway. Tall, thin, with a haughty face, she surveyed the harbour with a critical, disdainful glance. Her long fingers, laden with rings set with dark stones, pointed imperiously at the heap of her luggage — elegant little chests of ebony.

"It seems nothing ever changes here!" Her voice was thin and cold, like the edge of a razor. "You there! Yes, porter! Carry this over to that building. And don't shake it."

Sarang Kido came out of the ship's shadow. She looked at no one. Her gaze was empty and turned somewhere inward, but in that emptiness swirled such bottomless pain that the air around her seemed to vibrate. Now she simply stood, waiting.

The goblin Nogrom, by contrast, could not keep quiet. Stocky, his face covered with scars and soot, he loudly ordered about a pair of goblin underlings.

"Haul the crates over to those warehouses! Quick! Blockheads! See the ones with the red sign? That's explosives! Drop them and there won't be a wet spot left of you! Move!"

Nearby, already ashore, stood Anton and Julia Valir. A young man and a young woman, dressed in travelling clothes, but clearly of good quality. A sword hung at the young man's belt, and he carried it with accustomed confidence, but also with wariness. His eyes carefully surveyed the surroundings, assessing threats. Julia, his twin sister, was his complete opposite. She looked at the same scene with the haughty, bored curiosity of a princess who had wandered into a menagerie that smelled of dung.

The ritual of choosing began. Representatives of the four Orders stood at the four arches leading from the pier into the depths of the allod. Each new arrival had to go to the one whose philosophy was closest to them.

Priden Murk was the first to step forward. With a slight bow he passed to the arch under which Klavdia stood. After him, exchanging glances, came the Sharps, led by Gerda, and quiet Waterlily. The Order of Creation had found its champions.

The goblin Nogrom headed for Catherina without hesitation. He believed not in abstractions, but in the power of mana, metal, and the mind able to harness them. Eurial, casting a last contemptuous glance at the harbour, followed him. She saw in Progress not the betterment of the lives of all the inhabitants of Sarnaut, but a path to absolute magical might, to power over the very foundations of the universe.

Skulnar the Fierce walked up to Zayan with a slow, heavy tread. He did not bow, did not make speeches. He only looked the Master of Balance in the eyes and nodded. Zayan answered in kind. They understood each other without words. At a deep, intuitive level, the orc sensed the value of balance, the fragility of the universe. After him, Anton Valir also went to Zayan's arch. His idealism, his bookish yearning for justice and order inclined him to see in Balance the embodiment of law and reason.

Julia Valir lingered. Her gaze slid over her brother, over the stern arch of Balance, and a crooked smile appeared on her lips. She turned sharply and headed for the fourth arch. To Hirkalla. Where shadow reigned. The forbidden drew her. She saw in the Mysteries not a threat, but a source of personal power, a way to defy convention and the dull life that had been prepared for her. Almost at the same moment, like a soundless shadow, Sarang Kido moved towards Hirkalla as well. For the Arisen Zem, Darkness and mysteries were nothing alien. Darkness was their natural element, the only thing that did not condemn their pain, did not demand explanations of them.

Anton Valir was hailed almost at once.

"Prince! Valir!"

Anton turned. A man in plate armour bearing neither coat of arms nor ornament was approaching him. The eyes of the man who had called out looked calm and tired. But in that tiredness was the strength of forged steel.

"Paladin Belov?" Anton bowed his head respectfully.

"Gradimir," the warrior corrected him. "Titles do not matter now. Your choice… was expected. But do you understand its price? Balance is not just a pretty word. It is a duty. The duty to be the one who stands in the middle. Who takes the blow from every side. Who will be hated by friend and foe alike, because he keeps them from falling into extremes. Are you ready for that?"

"I am ready to serve order," Anton said firmly.

"Order is not yet balance," Gradimir shook his head. "Order can be tyranny. Balance is always dynamic. It is the art of being able to fall, yet not fall. Remember that."

Anton nodded, impressed and a little frightened by the warrior's seriousness.

Not far off, the elf Stephane de Doucer, leaning elegantly against a wall, was watching Eurial. An artist and sculptor, he regarded everything around him as potential material.

"Progress?" he remarked mockingly on her choice, noticing her haughty glance. "My dear, the most progressive invention of thinking beings is art. Everything else is vanity, dust and decay."

Eurial did not even deign to look at him, walking past as though he were empty space.

The Aoidos Cassandra, the seer, was crossing the square. Eyes lingered on her — looking for a sign, a hint, a prediction. Skulnar the Fierce approached her. He asked nothing, simply stopped beside her. Cassandra turned her face to him and slowly shook her head.

"The path is predetermined, orc," she whispered. "But the end… there are many ends. Go. Your fate is not yet written. Not here, and not now."

A valkyrie in shining armour crossed the bridge. Randgrid. Her gaze was heavy and appraising. She looked at the gathering not as participants in a tournament, but as potential warriors for some other, great battle. And, it seemed, she was left dissatisfied.

A pigeon perched on a high cornice of one of the buildings. An unusual pigeon. It was Georgi, one of Zayan's many guests.

"Games of the mighty," he clucked under his breath. "A tournament of ambitions. I wonder who will lose first? Or… who will be sacrificed first? What a line-up… Murk with the Sharps — an explosive mix. The elf with the goblin — a masterpiece, no less. And these young Valirs… the brother to order, the sister to chaos. Pure drama. I wonder what this smells of? It smells of big money on the bets…"

Friction was already beginning in the camp of Progress. Zarida Zharova, Catherina's young but promising pupil, was trying to establish discipline.

"Catherina ordered us to set up the measuring equipment first and…"

"Stop dawdling!" Nogrom barked, interrupting her. "First we set up the workshop! Without tools all your equipment is useless junk! Hey, you, horned goat, where are you taking that crate?!"

Eurial, meanwhile, ignoring them both, was already dictating something to her silent servant, pointing at the building where, in her opinion, her apartments ought to be.

The teams finally dispersed to their wings, which led deep into the allod. Anton, passing down a long, deserted corridor, saw a familiar figure ahead.

"Julia!" he called to his sister.

She slowed her step, but did not stop. She glanced over her shoulder. Mockery and slight irritation could be read in her eyes.

"Well? What do you want, brother? Running to preach a sermon about duty and honour?"

"Just… be careful," Anton said. "You have chosen a dangerous path."

"I have chosen an interesting path," Julia retorted. "And your 'safe' path leads straight to boredom and oblivion. Don't worry about me. Worry about yourself instead."

She turned sharply and disappeared around the corner; for a moment her shadow merged with the general darkness of the corridor.

Skulnar the Fierce fell behind his group. He needed to be alone. The crowd, the voices, the eyes of strangers — all of it weighed on him. He went out onto a small open balcony overlooking the unnaturally regular streets of the allod and the alien, unfamiliar sky overhead. The stars here were arranged in other patterns, cold and soulless.

And suddenly the wind died down. The hum of the flywheels stopped. An absolute, unnatural silence fell. Even the air ceased to tremble. The stars froze, and then their perfect lines began to warp. To weave themselves into chaotic, painful patterns.

Skulnar's heart clenched. Dreams of the future had already visited him seven years before. He already knew this feeling. The feeling of the Void approaching.

A vision swept before him. But it was not an image, not a picture. It was a sensation. The sensation of complete, absolute erasure. Not death. Not destruction. Not being turned to dust. It was worse. It was… non-existence. As if reality itself, the very fabric of being, were beginning to come apart at the seams, and through those seams Nothing was seeping. Without form, without colour, without sound. Simply… the absence of everything. An eternal, silent, all-consuming negation.

The tournament? The intrigues of the Orders? The arguments about Progress and Creation? All this fuss, all this bustle of mighty fools — it was pitiful, insignificant babble at the edge of an abyss that would not even notice their disappearance.

The vision broke off as suddenly as it had begun.

He heard scraps of an old man's voice, smelling of the dust of forgotten tablets and of madness: "…and I saw the Edge… not the edge of the land, but the edge of all that is… and beyond it — not darkness, for darkness is… but nothing, which is not… and it thirsts to become, by devouring that which is…"

And at once that voice was swallowed by a swelling, deafening… silence. A silence louder than any thunder. The silence of the Void.

The vision released him as suddenly as it had overwhelmed him. The wind rustled again, the flywheels started working, the stars stood in their perfect places.

Skulnar the Fierce leaned on the parapet, his hands trembling. He was breathing heavily, as after a long chase.

He looked at the beautiful, orderly, rational allod of Ney. At the towers where the champions slept or prepared for the tournament, dreaming of victory, of glory, of proving their Heads right.

He saw their internecine dispute, their ambitions, their games.

And he understood. All of it — the tournament, the Orders, the feuds — was nothing. Children's squabbling on the deck of a ship that had already sprung a leak and was being drawn relentlessly into a whirlpool.

The Void was coming.

Skulnar the Fierce remained standing alone, gazing into the perfect, false sky. Now he carried the burden of this knowledge. And he did not know to whom, or how, he could pass it on.

Anxiety, cold and clammy, gripped his heart. The foreboding of a catastrophe that made everything around him petty, insignificant — and therefore infinitely important. For now even the smallest victory might hide a chance of postponing the end.

## Part 3

### Skulnar's Vision

The city froze in November. A raw, colourless murk had enveloped it since early morning and did not let go until the next morning. It was not that picturesque, snowy November with a frost that nips your cheeks, but a dreary slush that chilled you to the bone. The streetlamps glowed a bewildered yellow in the wet haze, their blurry cocoons of light reflected in endless lake-sized puddles in which soggy cigarette butts and scraps of newspapers with unfulfilled hopes floated. From the gateways came a damp cold and a sourish smell, and from there drifted scraps of other people's conversations and the hum of a distant, alien life. A winter that had come not with frost, but with gloom.

In one of the five-storey blocks, behind a fogged-up, blind window, in a tiny kitchen steeped in the smell of life, there was an atmosphere all its own. Here, in an oasis of yellow light from a chandelier with plastic pendants, it smelled of cheap tea with a lemony sourness, of apple peel, of buckwheat porridge from last night's supper, and of thick, pungent cigarettes. On the table, covered with an oilcloth whose roses had faded almost to ghosts, stood a pot-bellied aluminium kettle, already cooling, giving up its last warmth to the palms.

Four of them. They gathered here on Wednesdays, as if concluding an unspoken pact against the darkness outside, against the winter advancing beyond the thin wall.

Oleg was stirring the dregs in a faceted glass with a teaspoon. The granulated sugar at the bottom crunched quietly and cosily. He wore a threadbare, scratchy sweater with reindeer on it, bought back in a cooperative shop in the days when such a thing was a novelty, a sign of another, almost fairy-tale life. An engineer by training, a programmer at one of the first firms trading in something obscure and virtual, he was a keeper of myths. His real world was here, in the books that stood in stacks on the fridge and under the window, smelling of printer's ink. He talked of the quantum probabilities of universes, of how every choice creates a new reality, and his eyes burned with a strange, otherworldly fire that reflected the flame of the gas ring.

"You see, Anya," he was saying, addressing a girl in big horn-rimmed glasses that kept slipping down to the tip of her nose. "All magic is just code. Not a spell, but a command. We simply haven't learned to read it. It is written not on paper, but on the very fabric of space."

Anna, wrapped in a big knitted shawl the colour of lingonberries — which she had, no doubt, knitted herself — kept adjusting the needles and the ball of dark blue, almost black wool lying beside her on a stool like the tools of her quiet craft. She worked at the main city library, in the rare books department, and she always smelled of old folios, of light sadness and of mothballs. Her fingers, thin and nervous, sorted through invisible threads of their own accord.

"Code," she repeated, and a soft, weary irony sounded in her voice. "And what is fate, then? A preinstalled program? Yesterday I laid out the cards. You drew the Fool, Olezha. Reversed. You're setting out on a journey, but the wrong way."

"Cards are just psychological projection," Oleg waved it away, but a shadow flickered in his eyes. "You project your fear or your hope onto a random set of symbols. I'm talking about fundamental, mathematical laws. About the symphony, not the notes."

"Random?" Anna shook her head, and the lenses of her glasses caught the light, flashing for an instant. "Nothing is random. Everything is threads. One is pulled by predestination, another…" she glanced at the third participant of their evening, "by chance."

Maria Tikhonovna was languidly sipping cheap wine from a faceted glass. She wore something bright and silky, the colour of a ripe peach, utterly out of season, and a thin gold chain on her ankle glinted from under her worn slippers. Nobody really knew what she did. People said she "sorted things out", "knew people". She smelled of expensive but cloying perfume that masked a faint smell of sweat. She smiled with her head thrown back, and there was something lazy, all-understanding and a little pitying in her smile.

"Well, I've got nothing to do with it," she laughed huskily, and her hoarse laugh was unusual for this kitchen. "Personally, I just toss a coin. It all depends on luck…"

Everyone looked at the fourth. Gennady. He sat in silence, one leg crossed over the other, smoking and gazing into the fogged-up, blind window, beyond which there was only darkness. He was thin, wiry, with piercing, too-lively eyes in a tired, weather-beaten face. It seemed he saw not the reflection of the kitchen in the glass, but something beyond it, something distant and invisible to the others. People said he had just come back from some country at the far end of the East. It was hard to say. He brought back not souvenirs, but ideas. Strange ideas, corrosive as acid, that turned the familiar little world upside down.

"Funny," he said at last, and his voice was quiet and muffled, yet it drowned out every other sound, as though someone had suddenly switched off the soundtrack in the room. "You talk about worlds, about codes, about threads. You build your houses of cards in this… tin can." He jerked his head, indicating the cramped, cluttered kitchen, the kettle, the oilcloth. "And you don't see what's outside the window. You don't see the foundation."

"What's out there? Fog? Slush? The stink of petrol?" Oleg shrugged, but displeasure sounded in his voice. "A temporary inconvenience. We're discussing something greater here. Something eternal."

"Go on creating," Gennady smirked, and there was not a drop of merriment in his smirk, only endless weariness. "You're creating foam. Soap bubbles. Beautiful, multicoloured, shimmering with every colour of the rainbow. And I'm talking about what lies between them. About what was before and will be after. About what all this rests on and what it will turn into in the end."

"Into Non-being?" Anya jeered, running a finger around the rim of her glass and leaving a greasy streak on it.

"Yes," Gena answered in complete seriousness. "Into Non-being. Into silence. Into the primordial vacuum where there are no codes, no threads, no lucky coins. Where there isn't even this tea, this oilcloth, or us. That is the only indisputable reality. Everything else is a dream that you try to put in order, sort onto shelves, take apart screw by screw, because you're afraid to wake up. There will simply be the Void… and silence. No stars, no planets, no black holes…"

An awkward, thick silence settled. A car could be heard driving past outside, its tyres slapping on the wet asphalt. Masha poured herself more wine, and the sound of the pouring liquid seemed indecently loud. Oleg frowned; his fingers clenched into a fist.

"That's too simple, Gena. Reducing everything to zero. Some kind of cheap nihilism. So in your view the authors of these books tried for nothing? Wrote all this for nothing? And all our arguments are about nothing? White noise?"

"Not for nothing," Gennady stubbed out his cigarette in a saucer with a puddle of tea, and it hissed. "It's beautiful. As beautiful and complex as the foam on the crest of a wave. But you'd have to be an idiot to think the foam is the ocean. You argue about the shape of the bubbles, about their size and strength, while I'm talking about true reality. Creating worlds? Fine. And I'm reminding you that they have an expiry date. They'll rot. Crumble to dust. They'll be gone. And it won't be a tragedy. It will be… natural. Like breathing out. Like a snowflake falling into a puddle."

"So everything is meaningless?" Anna asked quietly, almost in a whisper, and her fingers reached for the ball of wool, seeking comfort in it.

"No," Gennady looked at her, and suddenly a sort of tired, almost fatherly tenderness appeared in his gaze. "It isn't meaningless. It's… freedom. If there's nothing, there are no rules. There's no fate, Anya. There's only silence. And in it — absolute freedom to fall in any direction. You're afraid of that freedom. That's why you tie your knots, weave your nets of threads — just to have something to hold on to, just to feel a direction."
