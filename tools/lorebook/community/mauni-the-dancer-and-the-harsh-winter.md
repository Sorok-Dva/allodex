## I

Isa is a cold place! Blocks of ice float in the sea, a glacier creeps slowly down from the mountains, and ordinary snow falls several times a day. The people of this land are used to winter, because here it is always winter. But the astral currents are capricious, and sometimes summer comes to Isa along with the warm winds.

In summer the roofs of Skalgard begin to drip, and the little ones run through puddles instead of playing snowballs. But the grown-ups know that summer on Isa is the prologue to the harshest and most terrible winter. For soon the winds will change, and then the Fierce Frosts will come — a dreadful season when even your own fur is no help. In that month all the Gibberlings, with their penguins and squirrels, gather in one big house, carefully stop up all the windows and doors, and leave only one small hole for fresh air to come through. They eat their fill and then lie down to sleep — all together, side by side. This sleep is not quite ordinary, for they sleep not a day or two but a whole month! As soon as the frosts ease, they wake up hungry and scrawny and at once set about eating again…

This does not happen every year — perhaps once in ten years — and only the wise Thread Keepers, looking at the tapestry, can foretell when exactly the Fierce Frosts will come.

## II

Let us look at the streets of Skalgard… What time is this? Who knows. The Gibberlings do not keep such detailed chronicles as other peoples; for them all of history is a collection of sagas about the outstanding deeds of their ancestors. And it does not matter when they lived — a hundred, two hundred or a thousand years ago! Who would count?

Still, we know that not long ago Skalgard once again repelled an assault by the vile creatures of the Great Void, beings made of flame and mist, false beings that should not exist, whose Fate is not woven into the Tapestry. So the Gibberlings celebrate and make merry, for they have no enemies. And here at last is the long-awaited summer — never mind that the frosts will follow; what matters is the here and now!

Families run about, whole broods of sprouts! Loving brothers and sisters who know they will always be together. But look — not everyone is rejoicing. A little Gibberling girl stands frozen, all alone. Where is her family? Where is her sprout?

Ask any villager about her and you will get the answer: that is Mauni the Loner, Mauni the Troublemaker. She is always alone and always will be, because she was born that way.

Her ancestors must have done something wrong, must have angered Fate somehow, since their family has now split away from the common root of all Gibberlings — for Mauni's mother was a loner, and her grandfather too… That is what they are like, unusual, not right, and therefore one must keep away from them, because Bad Luck is the kind of thing that can stick to you in a flash. And Mauni is a good-for-nothing; the whole village knows it.

## III

No one wants to play with Mauni; no one lets her join their games. But she herself is a master at thinking things up: she loves all kinds of pranks and mischief. Dropping snow off a roof onto someone, or splashing water over a porch so that whoever steps out slips and lands right on their backside. For this, of course, they like her even less. And now the Thread Keepers announce that tomorrow the weather will change, which means it is time to prepare for the Fierce Frosts.

That evening there will be a great feast in Skalgard, and by the sea and in Honey Valley as well. Mauni went down to the sea — she had always loved that mysterious expanse. And though the sea nowadays is nothing like it used to be, she still feels some kind of call…

Leading her to faraway lands… What is it — the voice of the blood of her ancestors, who ploughed the open waters in their ships? Mauni does not know, but she walks along the shore, throwing the pine cones she has gathered at the snow crabs and gazing at the distant shore, visible through a transparent haze.

And though towards evening the grown-ups call the little ones together and ask them to find their brothers and sisters, everyone forgets about Mauni — perhaps by accident, perhaps on purpose. Little by little the sun sinks below the horizon, the sky darkens, torn by the runes that stretch from the Prophetic Ash, and the children, having run about between the villages, go to supper, after which everyone will get ready for the long sleep.

## IV

Mauni sits on the shore as if lost in thought; today for some reason she feels sad and lonely, even more than usual.

Suddenly she notices a little light somewhere far away, at the very edge of sight, at the rim of the ice, where the ice meets the Great Nothing. And so she rushes towards it, carefully jumping from floe to floe, drawn to this mysterious radiance that burns with a cold flame, that shimmers with every colour, from purple to icy blue, from bright green to magenta… And the closer she gets, the louder the song that comes from this little ball of light.

Carefully Mauni creeps to the very edge and, speaking to the little light, asks:

"Who are you?"

And the little light goes out for a second, as though embarrassed by the question. Mauni comes closer.

"Who are you?" she asks again.

"Me?" this cluster of every possible colour half whispers, half softly sings. "I never thought… I simply… am. I am a song…"

"Whose?" asks the astonished Mauni, who still does not understand how this being is talking to her. "Who is singing you?"

The little light flares brighter; it becomes something like an aurora, rising into the air and stretching out like a ribbon. It reaches out a streamer towards the Great Nothing.

"You are its song?" Mauni is amazed. "But there is nothing there."

But the song of the Nothing flares brighter still; now its colours change even more — red, yellow, green, blue and a thousand other shades that have no names.

"Everything is there," whispers or sings the little light. "Everything that could be… The unrealised… The possible…"

"Maybe my brothers or sisters are there too?" asks the little Gibberling girl.

"Perhaps…" The song sways above her. "Perhaps…"

"Oh!" Mauni remembers that it is already late. "I have to go! Bye." And with a wave of her hand she returns to the shore as quickly as she came.

## V

Silence reigns in the villages, for, having lingered on the shore, Mauni lost track of time. The sun set long ago, the little stars have come out in the sky, and the Gibberling girl is only now coming home. The wind over Isa has changed and the frost is coming — you can hear it: something cracks here, something crunches there. Glittering hoarfrost covers everything, wet branches stiffen, and thin ice seals the recent puddles, turning them into clear mirrors scattered all along her path.

Out of breath, she runs up to the gates — but hark! There are no guards, not a soul at all — the streets are empty, only here and there lie forgotten buckets, fish spears, even someone's toys. Coming closer, she picks up a forgotten doll from the ground and understands: everyone is there, inside, and they have forgotten her. And the Fierce Frosts are coming, which means the doors will not be unlocked tomorrow, nor the day after — for a long month, until the moon comes round again, she will have to wait here, outside.

Of course, she throws herself at the door, pounds on it with her little fists and shouts:

"Let me in! Let me in!" But it is no use, for the Gibberlings have already eaten and fallen asleep, and now they sleep so soundly that you could not wake them even if you kicked them.

Mauni turns her back to the door; she is frightened. Now she is here all alone, with night and the creeping cold all around, and no one to look after her.

## VI

The village is quiet; even the wind seems to have frozen. There is not a cloud in the sky, and the sky itself seems to have iced over — its colour is neither blue nor black but a kind of violet. All the light comes from the runes of the Ash, but even they seem to be starting to fade, yielding to the power of the cold.

Mauni's teeth are chattering, but then she sees a round moon rising over the mountains, not like the moon before. It looks like the huge eye of a fish that seems to be watching her.

"Oh, mama," the little Gibberling girl mutters and tries to hide.

But there is no hiding from the gaze of the monstrous fish. Its dead white pupil searches the snowbound village, looking for those who did not lock their doors behind them when the fierce cold came.

"Hey, you!" a voice rings out. "What are you rushing about for?"

Mauni looks around in bewilderment. It seems empty — everyone is asleep in their houses. Could she have imagined it?

"Don't just stand there like a post!" the mocking voice sounds again. "Come over here!"

And then the lost little Gibberling notices a creature sitting high on a tree branch. No wonder she had not noticed it before — it is white as snow itself, its legs and arms thin and like branches. Only its eyes stand out — like a lynx's, only much bigger.

"Who are you?" Mauni asks fearfully.

"Me?" the stranger bares her teeth in a grin, showing her fangs. "I am Hoo-Shaa…"

## VII

"Hosha?" Mauni tries to work it out, but in such frost even thinking is hard, as if thoughts freeze too. "And what are you?"

"Hoo-Shaa is Hoo-Shaa," the stranger remarks sensibly and jumps down. "Come on, quickly, before we meet the Isfrun…"

Mauni obediently takes the outstretched hand; this strange creature is smaller than she is, but her limbs are long, like her fingers. Her body is covered in thick grey fur, big ears stick up above her head, her nose is like a Heidrun's, but her face is wider and she has no horns.

The stranger pulls her upwards; she is strong and, clinging with her limbs, deftly climbs the sheer slope. All Mauni can do is puff and grab at the rocks, but Hoo-Shaa holds her easily and does not let her fall.

"Where are we going?" The little Gibberling girl lost her bearings long ago. And her companion now not only climbs ahead but leaps over crevices.

"To warmth." She is in a hurry, for the moon keeps watching them with its eye. So they must hurry.

Every living thing hides from the frost, taking shelter in burrows, dens and hollows. Only a little squirrel crosses their path. A funny little squirrel with a fluffy tail.

"Run to your mama, silly," Mauni tells him, stopping on a mountain ridge covered with pines.

But the little squirrel, having run off a little way, keeps staring foolishly at the two travellers climbing into the mountains.

At last they cross the mountain pass and, breathing out steam, crusted with ice, enter one of the caves, where water murmurs and life-giving warmth spreads.

## VIII

Hoo-Shaa draws the curtain of hides, sits down by the hot spring and says nothing — waiting for the cold to retreat and her bones to warm up. Mauni does not say a word either; she looks into the water, where a faint light rises from the depths, the glow of the heat underground.

She knows about the springs in the mountains, but the way they came is unfamiliar to her; she understands that they did not walk for long, but they went somewhere through the rocks into country where she has never been.

"Now we must wait," says Hoo-Shaa.

"Wait for what?" Mauni asks in surprise.

"For the Isfrun to pass…" Hoo-Shaa drops her voice to a whisper. "Have you really never heard of her?"

"The Isfrun," Mauni replies. "The ice maiden?"

"Yes." Hoo-Shaa moves even closer to the spring, as though talking about the Isfrun draws the warmth out.

"On days like these the ice maidens leave the Last Mountains, which lie far to the north. For they live there, in the abode of Helad, at the very heart of the Great Glacier. The Isfruns rush towards the sea, and when they reach it, they turn back again. And then the frosts go away… The ice maidens are beautiful, but you must not look them in the eyes, or you will turn into pure ice."

"But why have we never met anyone?" Mauni is puzzled. "Neither you nor them… If they walk about all the time… here, among us?"

"There are those who hide on ordinary days… But on days like these, unusual ones, they come out… While you sleep, this world belongs to them… Even if only for a short time."

"That's some kind of wrong world."

"Right or wrong, it doesn't matter." Hoo-Shaa frowns. "There is little enough of it as it is…"

## IX

A crack sounds beyond the curtain of the cave, as if a crust of ice were splitting under someone's footsteps.

Hoo-Shaa catches Mauni's curious look and, taking her by the hand, leads her to the entrance.

"Only keep quiet," she asks.

Outside the cave it is dark; the moon is still shining, but now with a different, less wicked light. The Ash has once again thrown out chains of runes that crawl across the sky.

Along the silvered path a white figure is moving away from them, but as soon as it passes under the shimmering of the heavenly glow it takes on the most varied hues — red, green, blue…

"The Isfrun," the mountain stranger whispers to the little Gibberling girl. But Mauni understands it herself. The tall ice maiden almost floats down the mountainside, leaving a strip of glacier behind her.

Right behind her the frost departs too, and it becomes easier to breathe.

They followed the path of the Isfrun — it is easy to see, a trail of smoothed ice, bright blue and gleaming. Some of the trees are also covered with a crust — perhaps they will never wake again, though who knows: the force of life often overcomes even the fiercest frost.

And then Mauni sees the little squirrel on the path. The very same little squirrel they had met on their way to the cave. He too is completely iced over.

"He looked into the Isfrun's eyes," says Hoo-Shaa. "And could not turn away."

"Poor little squirrel." Mauni takes him in her arms. "We must… at least bury him."

"I know a place," nods Hoo-Shaa. "By the Great Tree…"

And they go to the Tree. Around them is winter, but an ordinary one — not the monstrous winter…

## X

"Where did they come from?" Mauni is thoughtful and gloomy.

"Who?" Hoo-Shaa, who is carrying the squirrel, tilts her head and looks at her companion.

"Why, the Isfruns!" Mauni is angry now — at the ice maidens, at the squirrel, at the winter. "Where did they come from?"

"Have you really never heard of the Last Mountains?" her companion asks.

"Nope!" says the little Gibberling girl. "What mountains?"

"North of here," Hoo-Shaa tells her, "a vast plain begins. It is empty; nothing grows there, and all there is is snow and stone. Few go there, because it is very hard to find food there and, most importantly, the winds blow constantly and blizzards sweep across it. But if someone manages to cross the empty valley, they will find the Last Mountains. They say — though perhaps they lie — that these mountains were the home of the ice dragons, in the days before Num awoke… Back then terrible cold and dreadful heat reigned over all the land, and when Num worked his first wonders, these two forces, against his will, gave birth to two new gods… And in the Last Mountains the ice god, whose name is Helad, built his fortress…

"At first Helad had no servants, but as soon as the first of Num's children died of the frost, they at once went over to his side, as dead men… So now draugar, wights and Isfruns serve him. Helad chooses each of the ice maidens himself: he takes out her warm heart and puts a piece of ice in its place."

"How horrible…" whispered Mauni, even squeezing her eyes shut in fear.

"The Isfruns remember everything, but they no longer feel anything… They carry the bitter cold with them… Helad dreams that one day, when the world is whole again, his glaciers will once more flow down from the Last Mountains… And in the end the whole world will be buried under a mass of ice…"

"You shouldn't have told me that," Mauni said thoughtfully. "Now I'll have nightmares… But the world broke apart after the Great Yok, didn't it? And now Helad is locked up here, on Isa?"

"Who knows, little one," Hoo-Shaa nods. "Ice seeps into a tiny crack in a mountain and splits it apart. That is how Helad hopes to find his way… But here we are…"

They were standing at the foot of the Great Ash, where the ground had not frozen — the cold that had come could not touch it. There they buried the little squirrel.

Mauni, of course, returned to her village a couple of weeks later, when her kinsfolk woke up and opened the doors of their houses. All that time she spent with Hoo-Shaa, who introduced her to many other wonderful creatures living on Isa that the Gibberlings had never even heard of. But that is quite another story.
