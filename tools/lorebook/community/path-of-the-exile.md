## Part 1

The forests here breathed damp and old, mossy rot. Fog, like a grey wolf, licked the roots of the pines, and the path, winding between the bogs, led to a hamlet where huts shaggy with moss leaned over as though getting ready to lie down. Dakkar walked in silence, his raven-black cloak streaming behind him like a shadow in the light of the swaying lanterns. Behind the elf stomped Grimwald, a revived kobold with skin like cracked clay and beady eyes full of perpetual worry.

"Back to this rotten hole again, master?" The kobold tugged at the lead of a stubborn pony laden with bone amulets. "Last time they nearly wrung our necks! 'You'll call up the undead,' they said! And afterwards — 'Thank you, but why didn't you come sooner!' Ha-ha, typical!"

Dakkar did not turn. His face, pale and sharp, as if carved from moonlight, remained motionless. Only his golden lashes quivered:

"You can go back to the marsh toads, Grim. You like their chatter."

The kobold snorted and adjusted his sack of herbs, which smelled of death and wormwood:

"At least toads are honest. They don't jab a pitchfork in your back the moment the job's done..."

The village greeted them with closed shutters and whispers from behind fences. The headman, a fellow with a beard as tangled as the roots of a hundred-year-old oak, stepped forward, squinting with fear:

"D-did Tep bring you, sorcerer? Are the dead walking again?"

"No." Dakkar stopped and folded his arms across his chest. "I came to ask: do you need help or not?"

The crowd murmured. Grimwald climbed out from behind his master, baring yellow teeth:

"Fools! Who are you afraid of? Us?! We're kind... Well, almost. We shove the dead back into the ground so you can sleep in peace. And you — 'Oh, the cursed elf!' Why, without him you'd..."

"Shut up, Grim." Dakkar waved his hand. The kobold clutched at his throat, straining to squeeze out a sound, but only rolled his eyes. The elf turned to the headman: "The Witch's Bog. Who went in there in the last month?"

The crowd gasped. An old woman in a headscarf pushed forward a boy whose face was white with terror:

"Vanka... He was picking mushrooms! Go on, speak, you fool!"

"I d-didn't do anything!" Vanka fell to his knees. "I just... found a stone! A pretty one, with patterns! And then... voices..."

Dakkar sighed. It was always the same: curiosity, fear, a curse. A cycle as old as the world.

"Lead the way," he said curtly. "Before it gets worse."

The bog squelched underfoot as though it were alive. The fog clung to their clothes, whispering in a forgotten tongue. Vanka trembled, pointing at a half-flooded cave. At its entrance lay a stone covered in runes that looked like a tangle of snakes.

"Here..." the boy whispered. "They come at night... Skeletons in rusty mail. Guards, they say, of Prince Gostomysl..."

Grimwald snorted:

"Oh, these princes! They never get any rest. First they bury gold, then their bones come crawling out..."

Dakkar touched the stone. Cold pierced his fingers, and a howl rang in his ears. Dark magic — ancient, looped around pain.

"Leave," he ordered. "Both of you."

When their footsteps faded, the elf closed his eyes. Words of incantation, sharp as blades, crept through the air. The earth heaved. Shadows crawled out of the cave — skeletons with swords, entwined with marsh grass. Their eye sockets blazed blue.

"Servants of darkness!" Grimwald leapt behind a boulder. "Master, they'll..."

"Quiet." Dakkar stepped forward. His hands flared with black flame. "You are uninvited guests here. Time to go home."

The skeletons charged. The elf flicked his fingers — and the shadows beneath his feet came alive, weaving themselves into chains. The dead froze, bones creaking in the vise of darkness. Dakkar's spell swelled, mingling elven words with Old Kanian curses. The air rang like a taut string.

"Êav! Epägītæ!" his voice thundered. "Lie down, dust, and do not trouble the living!"

The skeletons crumbled to dust. The stone cracked, letting out a cry like a child's weeping. Silence.

Grimwald poked his head out:

"Is that it? Or is Baba Yaga about to crawl out next?"

Dakkar dropped to one knee, breathing raggedly. Dark magic drained his strength, but that was the price.

"The stone... was a guard's grave," he said. "His soul is bound to a treasure. Stupidity... breeds monsters."

The kobold ran up and pushed a flask of brew into his master's hands:

"Drink, or you'll die right here. And then who'll pay me for my suffering?"

The elf smirked. Always the same performance.

On the way back the village met them with silence. The headman thrust a bag of coins at Dakkar without looking him in the eye. The elf tossed it to Grim:

"Buy yourself a new jacket. That one stinks of the bog."

"Oh, such generosity!" The kobold giggled. "And you people, you could at least say 'thank you'! He just..."

"Don't," Dakkar said, turning toward the forest. "That's not what they pay for."

He walked away without looking back. Behind him came the whispers: "Sorcerer... undead... demons..." Grimwald caught up with him, muttering:

"And why do we help them? They'll never love us."

Dakkar stopped. Somewhere in the distance an oriole cried, as if mocking them.

"Love is for bards," he said. "Our job is to make sure they still get to sing their tales."

The fog thickened, swallowing the two figures. Somewhere in the thicket the wind began to howl — bony, cold, but already powerless.

## Part 2

The rain drummed on the roof of the Drunken Troll tavern so insistently, as if it meant to knock the memories out of his head. Dakkar sat in a corner, his fingers clenched around a goblet of cheap wine. The air smelled of wet wool, smoke and melancholy. At the next table, men were eyeing him askance and whispering: "Cursed elf... moves the bones..." Grimwald, his kobold servant, was gnawing a chicken leg and throwing them scornful looks.

"Don't be scared, blockheads!" he snorted, crumbs flying everywhere. "Tonight he's only moving the wine. And not very successfully at that."

Dakkar did not answer. The rain, the whispers, the smell of damp — all of it pulled him back. Back to the warm, light-flooded halls of the Magic Academy on the allod of Stellara. To where it all began.

Stellara. The Enchanted Grove of the Academy.

The young Prince Dakkar de Desirae walked along shining corridors of moonstone. Firefly spirits fluttered all around; elven youths and maidens in snow-white robes laughed. He was one of them — heir to a great House, a promising student of entropic magic. But another corridor beckoned to him. Narrow, dark, smelling of dust and old parchment. The door at its end bore the sign of crossed bones — the Archive of Forbidden Knowledge.

"Prince Dakkar?" The voice of Professor Edgar, ancient as the stones of Stellara, sounded behind him. "Only those beyond saving come here. Or those who think themselves stronger than the prohibition."

Dakkar turned. The old mage's eyes were like two cracks in a cliff — dark, impenetrable.

"I want to know, Professor. Why do we fear this part of magic? Why is death taboo? Isn't understanding the end the key to understanding life?"

Edgar smirked, his dry face wrinkling:

"Cynicism, in one so young. But... come in. Show me what your royal mind is capable of."

Dusty folios came alive under his fingers. The Scrolls of Tep, the Chronicles of the Shadowy Flame, the Dialogues with the Abyss. The magic of death turned out not to be filthy fiddling with bones, as nannies used to frighten children. It was... the geometry of the soul. Precise, cold, flawless in its logic. He saw the astral threads binding spirit and flesh, and learned to untangle them and weave them anew. He found flaws in the protective circles of senior mages and corrected them with a thought. He understood.

But understanding brought no honour. Whispers followed him:

"Necromancer..."

"Carrion-eater of souls..."

"Defiler... The shame of House de Desirae!"

Even his friends drew away. Their faces, once open, now twisted into a mask of disgust. The magic of the Elements was pure and noble. The magic of Death was the lot of pariahs.

The throne room of the Desirae palace. Five years had passed.

Dakkar stood before his father. King Louis de Desirae sat on a throne of pale elven oak, yet seemed carved out of ice.

"The best student of the Archive of Forbidden Knowledge," Louis's voice was quiet, like a dagger sliding over silk. "Professor Edgar's pride. The shame of our House."

"I did not desecrate graves, Father. I studied. I am in control."

"Control?" Louis rose, his robe rustling like snakeskin. "People don't see control, Dakkar! They see fear. They see bones stirring in the dark. They see a violation of what is most sacred — the rest of the dead. You will be an outcast. A curse. Rumours will fly after you like crows after carrion. No one will offer you a hand or call you friend. Only fear and hatred. You will doom yourself to loneliness. And our House — to contempt."

The cold words fell like stones. Dakkar felt his own pride — cold, elven pride — turning into armour.

"So House Desirae prefers ignorance to knowledge? Fear to strength?" he asked with icy courtesy.

"We prefer survival, son. Renounce it. Burn those books. Take up the magic of light. Or..." The King did not finish, but the unspoken words hung in the air: Or leave.

The door to a side gallery opened a crack. Lucretia de Desirae appeared. She smelled of expensive imperial perfume and of something foreign — the scent of her human lover, a general of the Empire, of whom the court whispered. Her eyes, golden and sharp as amber blades, slid over her son, then over her husband.

"Louis, stop preaching," her voice was sweet as poison. "The boy has found what he is strong at. Power, even dark power, is always useful. Especially when it is feared." She went up to Dakkar and touched his cheek with cool fingers. "Go your own way, prince. Let them hiss. Fear is power too. And power... is always right."

Her words were not support. They were a bargain. His power — her instrument. But at that moment, against his father's icy condemnation, they sounded like the only acknowledgement he had.

The present. The Drunken Troll tavern.

Dakkar sipped his wine. It was sour, like his thoughts. He had left that same night. Not as a prince, but as an outcast. Stellara, its shining spires and its hypocritical sages, stayed behind. His father had been right: they feared him. Despised him. Spat at the sight of him. His mother... his mother used his talent in her intrigues...

"Master?" Grimwald nudged him with an elbow, breaking the flow of bitterness. "The men say the will-o'-the-wisps are wandering on the bog again. The dead, probably. They whined, but they're promising money. Shall we go? Or do we run into pitchforks again?"

Dakkar stood up. The raven-black cloak fell onto his shoulders heavily, like armour. He tossed a coin onto the table.

"Let's go, Grim." His voice was dull, stripped of elven melody. "Someone has to put the bones to sleep. Even if the pay is fear and copper coins."

He stepped out into the rain. The wind howled in the chimneys like a restless spirit. Ahead lay the bog, the cold, the hostility of the living and the eternal rustle of bones underfoot. He had chosen this path himself. The road of forbidden knowledge. The road of fear. The road where the only reward was the silent repose of the dead and the bitter knowledge that his father, in his blind concern for reputation, had foreseen everything. But he walked it. Because someone had to. Because this was his choice. His curse. His duty. His lonely, bony truth in a world woven of light, lies and fear.

## Part 3

The silence of the bog was deceptive. The wind stirred the reeds, making a sound like the creak of ungreased cart wheels. Dakkar stood at the edge of the mire, where black water merged with the grey sky. Before him, within a ring of charred stumps, three figures were stirring. Not ghouls, not skeletons — worse. Rotlings. Creatures moulded from slime, rotting roots and the remains of the drowned. They squelched, stretching gelatinous tentacles toward a bound girl on a tiny islet in the middle of the quagmire. Her weeping was quiet, hopeless.

"Hey-ho, master!" hissed Grimwald, hiding behind the elf and tugging at his cloak. "Those are rotlings! They stink worse than my socks after a week in boots! Maybe we go around? Look, the girl's already half in the mire... We won't pull her out, and we'll sink ourselves."

Dakkar did not answer. His fingers were already tracing intricate signs in the air; cold magic thickened around them, freezing the drops of water on his cloak. He could hear them — faint echoes, distorted by pain, of the souls walled up in this rotting flesh. A silent cry for the torment to end.

"Æenebricolŧ šöv æmbrû Ţnvolvänï!" his voice, low and commanding, cut through the chorus of the bog. The shadows at his feet came alive, twisting into cords black as tar. They darted at the rotlings, sinking into their gelatinous bodies.

At that moment, he stepped out of the thicket.

A man in a priest's robes, snow-white and incredibly clean for these parts. On his chest, a shining symbol of the Light. A young face framed by a dark beard radiated fanatical conviction. In his hands, a staff with a crystal that gave off a soft, warm light.

"Stop, defiler!" the priest's voice boomed, making Grimwald jump. "Step away from the innocent and from these spawn of corruption! The power of the Light will lay them to rest!"

Dakkar did not even turn around. His cords tightened, drawing puffs of black vapour out of the rotlings — concentrated pain and hatred. The creatures howled, writhing.

"You're late, righteous one," Dakkar tossed over his shoulder without interrupting his work. "They're no longer 'spawn'. They're undead. They need peace, not your end-of-the-world light show."

"You speak to them in the tongue of Darkness!" The priest raised his staff. The crystal flared brighter, blindingly. A beam of pure light struck the nearest rotling. It shrieked and hissed, its rotting flesh smoking, but... it did not die. It only writhed in even greater agony. The girl on the islet cried out in terror. "You see?! Your vile magic only increases their suffering!"

"No," Dakkar answered coldly. "Yours prolongs it. Ç̈h Ťuollâṭ ê Œđïm!"

The elf's last command was like the crack of a whip. The black cords clenched into fists of darkness. The rotlings did not crumble into dust. They... collapsed inward. Silently, swiftly, like soap bubbles. Leaving only puddles of black slime and a quiet, almost grateful sigh, carried off by the wind. Dakkar's magic did not kill — it untied knots, freeing what had been held by force.

He stepped across a suddenly hardened shadow to the islet and cut the ropes on the girl's wrists. She stared at him in horror, crawling away.

"Run," he said curtly. "Before the bog changes its mind."

The girl took off and raced toward the village. The priest came up, his face burning with anger and righteous revulsion. His white robes were spattered with black mud.

"You... you're a sorcerer! A warlock!" He shook his staff in Dakkar's face. "You used the forces of Darkness before the eyes of a servant of the Light! I, Father Hilarion, accuse you of dark sorcery and of consorting with unclean spirits! You will stand before the headman's court!"

Grimwald climbed out from behind:

"Oh yes, oh yes! And who saved the girl, eh? Who calmed down those stinkers? Your glowing lantern only fried them like sausages on a fire! And the master — bam, done! Clean, neat..."

"Silence, creature!" Hilarion turned crimson. "Your 'cleanliness' is a stench in the nostrils of the Light! Bind him!"

Two hulking village lads with pitchforks came out of the bushes. They looked at Dakkar with a mixture of fear and hatred, fuelled by the priest's speeches. The elf sighed. He could have scattered them with shadows. But then he would have become the very monster they took him for. He lowered his hands.

"All right, Grim," he told the kobold. "Don't struggle. Let's play at being prisoners."

The prison in the village of Rotstump was simple: a damp cellar beneath the tavern, with bars instead of a door and straw on the floor that smelled of urine and despair. Dakkar sat leaning against the cold stone. Grimwald paced the cage like a cub in a trap.

"Idiots! Blockheads!" he hissed toward the bars, behind which one of the lads stood guard. "His Holiness pisses light — and everyone's delighted! And us — into the pit! I'll show them... I'll grow them such mushrooms in their vegetable patch that..."

"Be quiet, Grim." Dakkar closed his eyes. He could hear it — far away, beyond the walls. A dull, rhythmic thudding. As though someone were hacking at the earth with a blunt axe. Many someones. "They're coming."

"Who?" The kobold froze.

"Those whom your bright friend could not, or would not, truly lay to rest." Dakkar opened his eyes. There was no fear in them. There was a weary readiness. "The old graveyard beyond the outskirts. The earth there is... restless. And it smells of bitter wormwood. A familiar bitterness."

Grimwald turned pale (as far as that was possible for his greenish skin):

"The witch? The one the women at the well were chattering about? Darya the Sorceress, who ran off into the forest after a bear m****d her sweetheart on a hunt?"

Dakkar nodded. The thudding grew louder. Now it was joined by grinding, howling and... a quiet, piercing laugh drifting on the wind. A laugh full of madness and revenge.

Upstairs, in the tavern, panic broke out. Shouts, stamping, the clatter of broken crockery. Father Hilarion was shouting something about faith, about the power of the Light, about prayer. His voice was trembling.

Then the cellar wall opposite the bars exploded.

Not with the roar of dynamite, but with a ghastly, wet squelch, as if a huge abscess had burst. In poured streams of liquid mud, bones, scraps of shrouds and... them. Dozens of them. Skeletons in rotten rags, ghouls with shreds of skin on their bones, something huge and shapeless woven from grave clay and roots. In front of them, standing on a heap of rubble, was she. Darya. Or rather, what she had become. Her face a mask of madness, her eyes burning with green fire, her hair living snakes of marsh grass. In her hand, a bone staff crowned with a glowing skull. She smelled of death and wild wormwood.

"The liiiiiiiiiiiiiiving!" rasped a voice that sounded like hundreds of voices at once. "Pay your debt! For Pyotr! For all of them!"

The guard at the bars screamed, wetting himself, and dashed for the stairs. The ghouls rushed after him.

Dakkar stood up. Calmly. Even too calmly. His hands were already moving, tracing complex, swift runes in the damp air. The darkness answered him immediately, greedily.

"Šuḷguríḅ ŝīrtȧ æinimairef!" his voice rumbled like a thunderclap in an enclosed space. "Lie down!"

Black lightning, cold and silent, struck from his palms. It did not burn — it destroyed bonds. The skeletons that had been about to tear the fleeing guard apart crumbled into dust, as if mown down. A ghoul, its claws already sunk into the steps, froze; its vicious snarl gave way to a sudden, almost peaceful empty gaze before it collapsed, turning into a harmless heap of bones and rags. The clay monster wavered; its shape began to run.

The witch shrieked with rage. Her staff swept up, releasing a wave of green, venomous flame. It struck the black barrier that Dakkar instantly raised in front of himself and Grimwald. The fire hissed, breaking against the impenetrable darkness.

"You!" howled the witch, her green eyes blazing with hatred as she recognised in him someone who understood. "Dark one! You're in the way!"

"Calm yourself, Darya," said Dakkar, his voice strangely soft, yet carrying undeniable authority. He stepped forward through his barrier as if through water. His hand, wrapped in black radiance, reached for the staff. "Pyotr is long gone. His pain has ended. Rest, you too. Ȇcēïuq ẗe ȇcēïuqer. Peace."

He touched the bone pommel of the staff. The green fire went out. The witch's eyes widened; the madness in them wavered, giving way for an instant to an old, human pain and astonishment. Then the light in them died. She collapsed soundlessly, like a rag doll. Her body began to decay rapidly, turning into dust and marsh slime. The remaining undead, deprived of their mistress's will, froze and fell apart into bones.

Silence. Only moonlit dust floated in the dusty air, streaming in through the hole in the wall, together with the smell of decay, quickly driven out by a fresh, cold wind from the bog.

Upstairs the shouting died down. Pale faces appeared on the stairs. The headman, Father Hilarion, whose white robes were torn and smeared with soot, several men with axes. They looked at Dakkar, standing among the heaps of crumbled bones and dust, at the ruined wall, at Grimwald calmly chewing some root or other. The fear in their eyes was the same as ever. But now something else was mixed into it. Shock. Incomprehension. Shame.

"You... you..." Father Hilarion began, trying to find his anger, but there was only emptiness in his voice. His staff no longer glowed.

"Freed us," muttered the headman, looking at the hole in the wall. "By a miracle..."

"Not by a miracle." Dakkar brushed the dust off his sleeve. His voice was tired, as always. "By work. Dirty, dark, but necessary." He looked straight at Hilarion. "Your light is beautiful, priest. It warms souls. But it doesn't put out darkness. It only shows where it is. Putting it out... takes other methods. Sometimes, dirty hands."

He stepped across the pile of bones toward the breach in the wall. Grimwald hurried after him.

"Stop!" shouted Hilarion, but his voice faltered. "You... you're still a sorcerer! A dark one!"

Dakkar did not turn. He stepped out into the cold night air. The moon lit up the ruined village, the cries of the wounded, the traces of chaos. And the graveyard beyond the outskirts, now finally at peace.

"Yes," he said into the emptiness, knowing the priest could hear. "I am a sorcerer. A dark one. And as long as there are Daryas like this, graveyards like this, and priests who treat with light the burns from the torches used to burn witches... my work will not be done. Pay or not. Thank me or not. Fear me or not."

He walked away from Rotstump, his black cloak merging with the night. Grimwald glanced at him sideways:

"And the little bag of money? At least for the girl from the mire? Look, the headman's still shaking..."

"Leave it, Grim." Dakkar quickened his pace. "Here they'll pay in fear and whispers behind our backs. There's always enough of that. In other places... in other places they might give us a copper. For wine. Something to wash down this... bright truth."

They dissolved into the pre-dawn mist, leaving behind them the village, the ruins, the priest clutching his extinguished staff, and the bitter aftertaste of understanding that their saviour was the very one they had thrown into the pit. And that the clear light of day, soon to come, could never have done what the cold, merciless darkness had done.

## Part 4

The road was a grey ribbon winding between boundless, sullen forests and bogs that squelched along the verges like hungry beasts. Dakkar walked unhurriedly, his black cloak merging with the dusk that came early in these northern lands. Grimwald trudged behind, grumbling about the raw wind and his chafed paws. The silence was broken only by the creak of wheels and the clank of armour drifting from around the bend.

A detachment emerged from the fog. Some ten guards in sturdy, if worn, mail, with grim faces. In the middle, a covered wagon with a coat of arms on its side: a rearing silver stag on a gules field. Beside the wagon, astride a black stallion, rode a man of about forty. A prince. It showed in everything — in his straight bearing, in the hard gaze of his grey eyes, in his expensive but practical fur caftan. Adrian Valir. Dakkar recognised the arms.

The wagon's curtain was drawn back. A face peered out. Young, dazzlingly alive in the middle of the dreary landscape. Chestnut curls escaping from under a little fur cap, large dark eyes full of curiosity and a drop of fear. Full lips parted in surprise. Svetlana Valir. Eighteen years old, and her whole lush, alluring figure, generously outlined by a warm travelling fur coat — broad hips, high bosom, slender waist — seemed a challenge to the greyness and cold. She saw Dakkar — a tall, pale elf in black, with a detached face and a strange aura of cold — and started, recoiling inside.

"What's this obstacle?" Prince Adrian's voice was sharp as the crack of a whip. He reined in his horse, looking Dakkar and Grimwald over with an appraising, unfriendly gaze. The guards grew wary, their hands resting on their sword hilts. The kobold instantly darted behind Dakkar's back.

"The road belongs to everyone, my prince," Dakkar answered calmly. His elven accent made his speech melodious, but cold. "We are going our own way."

"By the look of you, your way leads straight to the gallows," Adrian said through his teeth, looking contemptuously over Dakkar's modest clothes. He did not see the signs of the royal House of Desirae hidden beneath the cloak. He saw a strange, possibly dangerous vagrant. "And what is that creature hiding behind you?"

"I am Grimwald, the venerable servant of the noble lord Dakkar, tamer of the unclean and connoisseur of ancient runes!" blurted the kobold, sticking his head out. "And you, I see, are the local prince? Travelling with your daughter? A pretty girl, no denying it! A real apple!"

"Silence, freak!" barked one of the guards. Svetlana peeked out again; her gaze slid over Dakkar with frightened interest.

"Father," her voice was melodious, like the chime of a little bell. "The forest ahead... They say there are robbers there. Perhaps... perhaps they could come with us? Just in case?" She nodded toward Dakkar. Her eyes met his silvery-cold ones. She quickly looked away, blushing slightly.

Adrian winced. The forest — the Blackwood — did indeed have a bad reputation. Several merchant parties had vanished there without a trace. The thought of letting a suspicious elf and his rat-like servant into his retinue repelled him. But the sight of his daughter, her hidden fear... and a presentiment of something evil hanging in the air, made him nod with poorly concealed annoyance.

"Very well. Walk at the back. Try anything and I'll have you shot with crossbows like mad dogs. Understood, ragamuffins?"

Dakkar merely inclined his head slightly. Grimwald snorted: "Ragamuffins... well, well!" They joined the tail of the column.

The road plunged under the canopy of age-old pines and firs. The forest stood gloomy and silent. The sun's rays barely broke through the coniferous thicket, leaving ghostly patches on the ground. The air was thick, smelling of pine needles, rot and... something else. Metallic. Sour.

Svetlana kept drawing back the wagon's curtain. At first her gaze only brushed Dakkar furtively as he walked alongside. Then she began to ask questions, timidly, through the window:

"Elf... Dakkar, is it? Why are you... dressed like that? All in black? Is it... in honour of your magic?"

"The colour is practical," he answered drily. "It doesn't show dirt."

"And... do you really tame the unclean? Is it frightening?"

"Sometimes it's frightening for them. Sometimes for me."

Her laugh, ringing and unexpected, tore through the forest silence.

"You're strange! But interesting!"

She began getting out of the wagon to walk beside him, on the pretext of stretching her legs. Her movements were smooth and alluring; she caught his gaze, smiled, swayed her hips. She asked about the elven lands, about the stars, about magic. Her interest was sincere, mixed with youthful coquetry and a thirst for adventure. Dakkar answered with restraint, but his icy gaze sometimes softened, lingering on her radiant face, on the chestnut hair tied with a ribbon. Grimwald giggled under his breath.

Prince Adrian watched all this with growing anger. His daughter, a princess of the blood, flirting with some wandering elven sorcerer! At last he could stand it no longer. Reining in his horse sharply, he placed himself between Svetlana and Dakkar.

"Daughter! Into the wagon! At once!" his voice thundered, making the guards flinch. "You forget your dignity! And you," he turned to Dakkar, jabbing a finger, "keep away from her! Understood, ragamuffin? Your filthy magic and your thoughts are no match for her! One more loose word and I'll have your tongue torn out!"

Svetlana flared up: "Father! He didn't do anything..." But Adrian only shoved her roughly toward the wagon. She jumped inside, snapping the curtain shut. Tears of hurt glistened in her eyes. Dakkar met the prince's gaze. Not a shadow of anger, only a cold, bottomless calm.

"Your daughter asked questions. I answered. Nothing more, Prince Valir."

"Shut up and walk!" Adrian grunted, turning his horse. The forest around them was thickening, growing ever gloomier. That same sour, metallic smell grew stronger.

They found them an hour later. Or rather, found what was left.

First it was the smell. Not just blood — a whole slaughterhouse. Thick, sickly-sweet, hanging in the air like a dead fog. Then the flies. Myriads of them, buzzing in a black cloud. And finally, the scene.

A glade. It was littered with the wreckage of a smashed wagon and torn bales of goods. And bodies. Or rather, what had once been bodies. It did not look like the work of men or beasts. It was... mincing. Chunks of flesh, smeared entrails, shattered bones scattered like wood chips. The guards went pale; some turned away, holding back vomit. Svetlana, looking out of the wagon, screamed and fainted. Adrian jumped down from his horse, his face ashen, his hand gripping his sword hilt until his knuckles turned white.

"What... what Tep-cursed devilry is this?" he croaked.

Dakkar walked around the glade, his gaze cold and thoughtful. He felt the ground, examined shreds of clothing and broken weapons. Grimwald hid at his feet, trembling.

"Robbers," Dakkar stated. "The very ones who robbed the merchants. Apparently these were their last victims." He pointed to scraps of rich clothing under a wagon wheel.

"Who kills LIKE THIS?" one of the guards, apparently a sergeant, forced out.

Then came a groan. From under a heap of bloodied rags and splinters, a man crawled out. Or rather, what was left of him. His legs were gone below the knees, one arm had been torn off, his belly ripped open. His face, covered in blood and dirt, was twisted with inhuman pain.

"M-monster..." the dying man wheezed, his eyes, full of madness and horror, darting about. "B-bone... It woke up... She nodded, wildly, desperately.

"Then listen carefully..."

The plan was risky, almost insane. Hilarion's forces were great: a dozen guards, the militia, and he himself with his staff of Light. Breaking through by force would be suicide. What was needed was cunning. A diversion. And luck.

Luck arrived in the form of a carriage bearing the Valir arms, which stopped at the Three Bears inn just as Dakkar was leaving the prison. Out of the carriage stepped she. Svetlana Valir. In a travelling dress that emphasised her lush figure, her hood thrown back from her chestnut hair. Her eyes, large and dark, found Dakkar at once. Joy flared in them, mixed with alarm.

"Dakkar!" She almost ran up to him, paying no attention to the astonished passers-by or to the grumbling Grimwald. "Oh Tensess, it's you! Father and I are passing through, on our way to the capital... What happened? You're pale..."

He glanced around quickly and pulled her into the shadow of the inn's arches. He spoke quickly, clearly, without embellishment. About Hilarion. About Marya. About the coming execution. About his inability to act alone.

Svetlana listened, her face growing ever more serious. Fear gave way to anger, then to resolve.

"That... priest!" she hissed, clenching her small fists. "After you saved us in the forest! He dared?! And that girl... Marya... burn her?! No, Dakkar, it will not happen! What shall we do?"

The plan was born swiftly. He used what was at hand: Svetlana's status, her beauty, surprise and... Grimwald's particular talents.

Dawn painted the sky in dirty pink tones. The square in front of the town hall was filling with people again. A pyre had already been built in the middle. Hilarion stood on the platform reading a prayer, his face radiant with solemn cruelty. Marya, pale as death, in a dirty shift, was being led to the post. The crowd hummed like a disturbed hive.

Suddenly a luxurious carriage bearing the Valir arms flew into the square! It stopped abruptly, nearly running over a couple of onlookers. The door flew open, and Svetlana Valir stepped onto the footboard. All in velvet and furs despite the early hour, dazzlingly beautiful, with anger blazing in her eyes. Her ringing voice, strengthened by magic or simply by fury, rose above the hum of the crowd and Hilarion's prayers:

"Father Hilarion!" she thundered, pointing at the priest. "How DARE you?!"

Everyone froze. Hilarion broke off, his mouth left hanging open. Svetlana stepped down and walked toward the platform, her cloak streaming behind her like a banner.

"This girl is a distant relative of mine!" she declared, going up to the post and embracing the trembling Marya. The crowd gasped. "Marya Valir! Given to be raised in a remote village! She is accused of witchcraft? That is slander! An insult to the honour of the House of Valir! I demand her immediate release and an investigation into this lawlessness! Where is the headman?! Where is the burgomaster?!"

The confusion was complete. The Valir name, the princely arms, the fury and confidence of the beautiful princess — all this stunned the crowd and the guards. The headman, a fat fellow, climbed out of the crowd, stammering:

"P-princess... we didn't know... Hilarion said... a witch..."

"Hilarion was MISTAKEN!" Svetlana retorted, her eyes flashing at the priest. "Or is he deliberately defaming the honour of my family? My father, Prince Adrian, will be most interested to hear of it!"

Hilarion turned crimson. He saw the trap but could not get his bearings at once. A prince's daughter! An accusation of slandering a noble house! This was far above his sandals.

"Princess..." he began, trying to recover his righteous fervour. "Do not be deceived! The demon in her is cunning! It can take on the faces of kin..."

"Silence!" Svetlana cut him off imperiously. "Release her at once! Or I shall order my men..." She glanced meaningfully at the carriage, from which two hulking riders in Valir livery had already stepped — hired at the inn by Grimwald for an outrageous sum.

At that moment, while all attention was fixed on the performance on the platform, while the guards exchanged bewildered glances and Hilarion tried to shout something incoherent, Dakkar acted. Like a shadow he slipped to the back wall of the town hall. His fingers touched the stone. Ancient elven runes, a necromancer's quiet whisper — and the stone gave way, crumbling into fine rubble, opening a passage to the cellar. It was an old way in, which he had learned of from Grimwald, who had been trading in rumours around the town.

A minute later he was in the cell where Marya had just been. A minute after that he led Marya out, disguised in Svetlana's cloak — the likeness of their silhouettes in the turmoil did its work — through the hole in the wall into a dark alley, where Grimwald waited with two saddled horses.

They galloped away from Krutogorsk, leaving an uproar in the square behind them. Svetlana joined them at the edge of town. They stopped by a forest brook, beyond which began a wilderness outside Hilarion's reach.

Marya, still trembling but with hope now in her eyes, looked at her rescuers.

"Thank you..." she whispered, looking at Dakkar, then at Svetlana. "I... I don't know..."

"Go," said Dakkar, pointing to the forest path. "Go deep. Seek the Stump. Say that the Prince of Bones sent you. They will help you there. There your knowledge of herbs will be honoured, not burned at the stake."

The girl nodded, cast a last glance at the town where she had nearly been burned, and disappeared into the green thicket.

Svetlana came up to Dakkar. The morning sun played in her chestnut hair.

"You took a risk," she said quietly. "For her. For a stranger."

Dakkar gazed at the path down which Marya had vanished.

"Someone has to," he answered, an echo of his old thoughts. "Hilarion... he'll hate you now too."

Svetlana smirked, proudly raising her chin.

"Let him hate me. I am a Valir. I'm no stranger to the envious and to enemies." She paused. "And you... you'll leave again?"

Dakkar met her gaze. In those dark eyes was the same infatuation as before, mixed now with admiration for what he had done and sadness at the coming parting. He saw the road before her — bright, full of possibilities. And his own — dark, dangerous, lonely.

"My road always leads away, princess," he said, already turning toward his horse. "But... thank you. For your help. For... your courage."

He swung into the saddle. Grimwald was already waiting, holding his horse's reins.

"Goodbye, Dakkar," said Svetlana, and her voice trembled. "Be careful."

He nodded, unable to say more. The warmth of her gaze burned him more fiercely than any of Hilarion's pyres. He turned his horse and rode off into the forest without looking back, feeling her gaze follow him until he was out of sight. Grimwald rode beside him, muttering:

"Well, at least clever Marya grabbed a bit of the princess's gold for the help... Oops, I mean... a noble reward! For wine, master! To wash down all this human stupidity and... er... bright sadness!"

Dakkar did not answer. He was thinking of Marya's grey eyes, full of horror, at the post. Of the fanatical gleam in Hilarion's eyes. Of Svetlana's warm, loving gaze. The fire of fanaticism and the fire of feeling. Both could burn everything to the ground. But one only sowed death. The other... the other warmed, if only for a moment, his eternally cold road. He shook himself, pulling his hood lower. Ahead lay bogs, the restless dead and the eternal whisper of bones underfoot. His road. The road of the Prince of Bones. But now another small flame burned in his cold heart — the memory of chestnut hair and of a courage that dared to challenge a whole town for the sake of a stranger. And that little flame, it seemed, kept the darkness at bay a little better than any magic.
