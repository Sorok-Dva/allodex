Mayane woke suddenly in the middle of the night, raising her head and listening tensely to the darkness. The echo of the voice she had heard still seemed to ring in her ears. "You will never get what you desire."

But whose voice had it been? It seemed at once familiar and strange. Mayane felt uneasy; what had happened seemed important, yet she could not remember what her dream had been about.

On the neighbouring mat Yarune was tossing and turning. Her sister was dreaming too, and judging by the faces she was pulling, it was a nightmare as well.

Mayane tried once more to concentrate and remember. The only fragment that surfaced in her memory was a huge, dark wall of water crashing onto the shore. She had never seen waves like that, and where would they even come from?

The sense of approaching disaster kept her awake until dawn, which, to her relief, soon came. Stepping out of the yurt, she looked around. The village was waking; the other Heidruns were getting up too, peering into the morning sky and waiting for the Star of Fortune to appear, or for other omens promising good luck or bad. But this time the heavens were silent.

Mayane knew that her people worshipped luck, which, they believed, turned its gaze upon those it had come to love. And the best way to make luck love you was to live as if there were no tomorrow at all. So the Heidruns tried to live for the day and not to plan far ahead, for luck might turn away without warning. No one knows what tomorrow holds.

Now, however, that was not enough for her: the dream would not leave her mind. She knew that other peoples believed in prophecies, in visions of the future that could be interpreted correctly, so that something could be done in advance.

Of course, if you live in a green valley warmed by the flame of Muspel, where there are almost no troubles or worries, why would you think about the future? Even the raids of the Urun, sudden and cruel, seemed something inevitable, like a natural disaster. But beyond the valley cold winds blew, and there the ability to know the future mattered far more.

"Iriko," Mayane thought. The old sage could help. He saw much further than most Heidruns, being descended from Ilko himself, who in his day had answered the call of the other peoples and led his tribe into the war that granted them all long centuries of peace.

She found him where she expected. The old Heidrun stood motionless at the edge of the valley, staring intently into the distance.

"I had a dream," Mayane said quietly. "Something has happened in the world."

"Many things happen in the world, child," Iriko remarked without turning around. He had clearly been expecting this conversation.

"But can these visions be true? And what awaits us?"

"Our friends believe they can. Long ago Seida foretold the coming of the great Nothing. But there was also a part of her prophecy that remained unexplained."

"But Fate is treacherous!

Age will follow age,

Doom will come again,

Darkness grip the world.

I see it as if real:

The Tree is withering,

The ancient foe is woken,

The dead will find no rest!

A deadly cold will come,

And countless enemies,

Those whose hides are white,

I see across the ages."

Mayane recited from memory. Of course she remembered these verses. Everyone on this island knew them. "Have those times really come?"

"See for yourself, child," said Iriko, pointing upwards. Mayane raised her eyes. There, above the horizon, covering the heavens, rose the Great Tree, whose crown gave off shining Threads that embraced all the visible space and stretched far in every direction.

And with tears in her eyes she saw something else as well: a withered leaf, whirling and falling towards her.
